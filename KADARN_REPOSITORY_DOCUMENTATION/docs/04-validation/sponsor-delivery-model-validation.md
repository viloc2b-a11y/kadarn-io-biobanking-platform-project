# Kadarn Delivery Model for Sponsors, CROs, Vendors, Auditors, and Sites

## Executive judgment

The core idea in the draft is strategically sound: Kadarn should not be designed as a “document dump” product. The better framing is a **site and investigator intelligence delivery layer** built on top of a canonical evidence base, with documents exposed only when the use case actually requires them. That direction is consistent with current GCP thinking, which pushes sponsors toward risk-proportionate, operationally feasible processes that avoid unnecessary burden on investigators, and with industry efforts such as TransCelerate’s Site Qualification and Training and Shared Investigator Platform programs, both of which were explicitly created to reduce duplicate questionnaires, duplicate forms, and duplicate training across sponsors. citeturn14view2turn7search2turn15view0turn4view3

My conclusion is that the **best model for this block is a hybrid, progressive-disclosure architecture**:

1. a structured **Passport/Profile** for broad understanding of the site,
2. a **Capability and Readiness Matrix** for fast qualification judgments,
3. an **Evidence Package** for claim-level proof,
4. a controlled **Document Vault** for just-in-time document access,
5. a **Package Generator** that assembles study-specific exports for startup, feasibility, lab, investigator, or audit use cases.  

That is stronger than either extreme the draft poses—giving the full site passport and letting users browse everything, or giving only a static matrix—because regulators and industry standards both point toward role-based access, traceability, version control, auditability, and minimizing unnecessary burden. citeturn5view3turn5view4turn4view1turn14view2

## What the current proposal gets right

The proposal is right that different stakeholders need different levels of detail. FDA’s 1572 guidance makes clear that sponsors collect information about the investigator’s qualifications and the clinical site in order to establish that the investigator is qualified and the site is appropriate; ICH E6(R3) similarly requires that investigators be qualified, provide evidence of qualifications, and have adequate staff and facilities, while sponsors must oversee investigator and service-provider suitability using risk-proportionate approaches. That means a one-size-fits-all delivery object is mismatched to actual regulatory and operational needs. citeturn4view2turn14view0turn14view1turn14view3

The proposal is also right that **structured capability data** should sit upstream of documents. TransCelerate’s official Shared Investigator Platform materials describe a “Facility Profile Form” as a questionnaire for collecting site capability information, specifically to reduce the need to fill out duplicative forms for each sponsor or each new trial. Its broader SQT initiative says the same thing in different words: the purpose is to simplify qualification and reduce repeated, non-study-specific burden on sites. That strongly validates the idea that Kadarn should have a reusable structured profile layer rather than start with ad hoc PDF exchange. citeturn15view0turn7search2turn4view5

The proposal is especially strong on separating **claims** from **evidence** from **documents**. Current ICH E6(R3) emphasizes quality by design, critical-to-quality thinking, and processes that are operationally feasible, while the EMA TMF guideline expects document identification, version history, search, retrieval, metadata discipline, and traceability of any alteration. In practical product terms, that means a capability claim like “PBMC processing” or “overnight PK” should not simply be a sentence in a profile; it should be a navigable object linked to evidence, source documents, dates, and limitations. citeturn14view4turn5view4turn5view1

The proposal is also correct that a separate **Publication Engine** should exist. EMA’s TMF guidance explicitly expects role- and permission-based access, tight access arrangements between sponsors and CROs, restricted access to archived content, and auditability of access and changes. FDA guidance on computerized systems and 21 CFR Part 11 likewise requires secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails for changes to electronic records. Those expectations support a design where Kadarn’s evidence store remains canonical, while a separate access-and-packaging layer governs what is visible, downloadable, exportable, or time-limited for each actor. citeturn5view3turn4view1turn1search14turn6view3

## Where the proposal should change

The first change I would make is conceptual: the draft currently mixes **facility/site data**, **investigator qualification data**, and **study-specific startup packages** inside one conceptual stack. Industry practice treats these as related but distinct. TransCelerate distinguishes facility-level and department-level capability profiles, while FDA’s 1572 framework distinguishes investigator qualifications, research facility information, laboratory information, IRB information, subinvestigators, and financial disclosure requirements. Kadarn should therefore model at least three separate objects: **Site/Facility**, **Investigator**, and **Study/Protocol Package**. citeturn15view0turn16view1turn6view2

The second change is to avoid making the **Passport** the main delivery object. The Passport should be the entry point, not the payload. For most sponsor and CRO decisions, the first practical question is not “show me everything,” but “is this site fit for this protocol, and what is the evidence strength?” That is exactly the type of burden-reducing, risk-proportionate workflow encouraged by ICH E6(R3). So the Passport should stay high level and browsable, while the real operational objects are the Matrix, the Evidence Package, and the generated study bundle. citeturn14view2turn6view0

The third change is to redesign the **Capability Matrix**. The current example uses capability, status, confidence, and evidence count. That is a useful draft, but not sufficient for a buying or qualification decision. Because TMF and GCP expectations stress versioning, metadata, and ongoing oversight, the matrix should also show at least **freshness**, **evidence coverage**, **missing items/gaps**, and **last verified date**. In other words, a sponsor should not only see “PBMC: High, 92, 14 pieces of evidence,” but also whether the supporting certifications are current, whether the relevant SOP is current, whether a lab qualification is expired, and what the unresolved gaps are. That is an inference from the regulatory need for traceability, version history, auditability, and ongoing sponsor oversight. citeturn5view1turn5view4turn14view3

The fourth change is terminological. “Institution Passport” may be too abstract for the market. TransCelerate uses language like **Facility Profile** and **Department Profile**, and sponsors, CROs, and sites are already accustomed to those concepts. Kadarn can still use “Passport” as a product brand, but the external language should likely be closer to **Site Passport**, **Facility Passport**, or **Facility Profile** so buyers immediately understand it. citeturn15view0

The fifth change is prioritization. The **Historical Portfolio** and **Institution Digital Twin** are interesting differentiators, but they are not the most important design decisions for this block. The compliance-critical and buyer-critical work is controlled delivery, package generation, entitlement logic, document freshness, audit trails, and standards-compatible export. A digital twin becomes much more compelling after those foundations are solved. That prioritization is an inference from the fact that regulators focus on document integrity, retention, access, traceability, and oversight—not on immersive UI. citeturn5view3turn4view1turn1search1

## Recommended delivery architecture

The best architecture is a **five-layer delivery model** built on a canonical evidence core.

At the bottom is the **Canonical Evidence Core**. This is the source of truth for claims, source records, documents, metadata, provenance, contradictory evidence, verification state, dates, and ownership. It should store both structured facts and document references, and it should preserve traceability for any change. EMA expects version history, search and retrieval, metadata, and traceable alteration; FDA expects auditable electronic records. citeturn5view4turn5view1turn4view1turn1search14

Above that sits the **Policy and Publication Engine**. This governs role-based visibility, redaction, watermarking, export templates, expiry windows, NDA gates, and download permissions. EMA’s TMF guidance is very explicit that access should be based on defined roles and permissions, and that archived eTMFs should grant read-only access with an audit trail recording activity. That is not a cosmetic feature; it is a design requirement. citeturn5view3

The first user-facing layer is the **Passport/Profile Layer**. This is the browsable executive view of the site or facility. It should summarize identity, specialties, therapeutic experience, core infrastructure, research staff, accreditations, sample-handling and lab capabilities, historical research footprint, and “last verified” indicators. This is consistent with the kind of non-study-specific site capability information TransCelerate captures in facility and department profile forms. citeturn15view0turn7search2

The second user-facing layer is the **Capability and Readiness Matrix**. This should answer whether the site can support a given protocol or operational requirement, with visible evidence strength and freshness indicators. This is where sponsors and CROs should spend most of their initial decision time, because ICH E6(R3) emphasizes operational feasibility, critical-to-quality thinking, and avoiding unnecessary burden. citeturn14view4turn14view2

The third user-facing layer is the **Evidence Package**. This is the crucial feature. Each capability or claim should open into a structured dossier containing the claim, supporting evidence, source links, document pointers, contradictory signals, missing elements, dates, and an explainable scoring model. That structure is much closer to how sponsors, CROs, and auditors actually assess site suitability than a giant undifferentiated folder. It is also more aligned with quality-by-design and traceability expectations than a static PDF. citeturn14view4turn5view4turn5view1

The final layer is the **Document Vault and Export Layer**. Documents should remain accessible, but on a controlled, just-in-time basis. If a sponsor needs a PI CV, medical license, CLIA certificate, calibration record, or SOP, Kadarn should expose the specific artifact with metadata and audit controls. If the user needs a startup bundle, Kadarn should generate it from templates and rules rather than relying on manual curation. That is the most natural way to satisfy both burden reduction and compliance expectations. citeturn15view0turn4view2turn5view2turn5view3

## What each audience should actually receive

For **sponsors**, the optimal default is not a full passport download. It is a protocol-oriented view: a Passport for orientation, a Matrix for qualification, and a study-specific Evidence Package or Readiness Package for decision support. ICH E6(R3) makes clear that sponsors must assess feasibility, ensure sufficient quality, and oversee investigators in a risk-based way; TransCelerate similarly focuses on reducing duplicated non-study-specific work. That combination argues for delivering decision-ready information first, full documents second. citeturn6view0turn7search2turn15view0

For **CROs**, the package should go one level deeper. They need the sponsor-facing qualification view, but they also need startup and handoff materials: 1572-related items, PI qualifications, facility/lab details, IRB routing information, financial disclosure status, training artifacts where applicable, and a visible gap list. FDA regulations require sponsors to obtain a signed 1572 before an investigator begins participation under an IND, to collect a CV or other statement of qualifications, and to collect sufficient financial information for required financial disclosure forms. That makes a generated CRO startup bundle a very sensible product surface. citeturn4view2turn6view2turn2search11

For **vendors**, the package should be narrower and highly operational. A central lab or biospecimen logistics vendor usually does not need the whole institution narrative. They need targeted capability proof: sample processing windows, centrifuges, freezers, temperature monitoring, chain-of-custody processes, shipping conditions, courier cutoffs, IATA training status, and relevant SOPs or certifications. This narrow delivery approach is consistent with role-based access principles and with minimizing unnecessary burden and document exposure. citeturn5view3turn14view2

For **auditors** and inspection-readiness users, the model should invert: not a curated summary first, but traceability first. They need version history, provenance, certified-copy status when originals have been replaced, read-only archive access where appropriate, evidence of who changed what and when, and a clear linkage between claims and source artifacts. EMA explicitly requires traceability of alterations, metadata discipline, access controls, and certified-copy controls for copies that replace originals; FDA expects time-stamped audit trails. citeturn5view2turn5view3turn4view1turn1search14

For the **site itself**, Kadarn should not only publish outward; it should provide a self-service internal readiness view. Because ICH E6(R3) expects investigators to provide evidence of qualifications and demonstrate adequate resources, and because sponsors must oversee sites and service providers, the site-facing dashboard should highlight expiring certifications, missing documentation, weak capability claims, and study-specific readiness gaps before anything is published externally. That internal use case is a logical extension of the same quality and oversight requirements. citeturn14view0turn14view1turn14view3

## How document delivery should work

The draft is right to propose a **Document Vault** rather than a bulk folder, but that idea needs to be hardened into a compliance-grade artifact service. Every document should carry metadata such as artifact type, owner, associated site/investigator, effective date, expiration date, version, visibility class, download permissions, and audit history. EMA’s TMF guidance explicitly says metadata may include creator, time and date of creation, archive date, location, file title or keywords, version, file type, and file size, and that metadata should be formally defined for consistency. citeturn5view1

Kadarn should also support **certified-copy logic**. If an uploaded electronic file is going to replace an original in the operational record, then the system must distinguish between ordinary copies and certified copies. EMA defines a certified copy as a paper or electronic copy verified or generated through a validated process to produce an exact copy with the same information, including context, content, and structure. That matters because many startup and audit workflows break if the Product UI treats all uploaded PDFs as equivalent. citeturn5view2

One especially useful implementation detail is that Kadarn should not overcomplicate CV handling. FDA’s 1572 guidance says Section 2 requires a CV or other statement of qualifications, but FDA does **not** require the CV itself to be updated during the study or to be signed and dated; the dated signature on the 1572 is sufficient to attest to the accuracy of the CV or other statement of qualifications submitted with it. That gives Kadarn room to design a simpler, lower-friction workflow for PI qualification documents, instead of forcing needless re-signing rituals that do not add regulatory value. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn16view2

Document delivery should also be **template-driven**. A sponsor user should be able to request a “Regulatory Starter Kit,” a “PI Qualification Pack,” a “Lab Capability Pack,” or a “Feasibility Response Pack,” and Kadarn should generate the package from the canonical store. That is much more scalable than asking the site to assemble manual ZIP files every time. It also aligns with the market precedent that structured site-entered data can drive derived forms and documents, as seen in Shared Investigator Platform materials that describe site information being harmonized from one source and used to support documents and workflows across sponsors. The strongest part of that precedent is not the exact vendor implementation; it is the proven demand for reuse and derived outputs from a canonical profile. citeturn15view0turn4view4

Finally, Kadarn should support **standards-compatible export** for sponsor and CRO systems. The CDISC/TMF community’s eTMF Exchange Mechanism Standard exists specifically to support exchange of TMF content between systems using an inventory file and validation mechanisms such as schema validation and checksum verification. If Kadarn eventually wants to push startup or inspection bundles into sponsor eTMF ecosystems, aligning export metadata to that model would materially improve interoperability. citeturn8search0turn8search1turn8search2turn8search7

## Governance, privacy, and audit requirements

The biggest hidden risk in this whole design is not UX. It is **controlled exposure of personal data and sensitive operational data**. Investigator CVs, licenses, certificates, agreements, financial disclosures, and certain site records all raise privacy, confidentiality, or commercially sensitive handling questions. EMA’s current CTIS framework explicitly distinguishes what can be made public and what must remain protected for personal-data or commercially confidential reasons, while the TMF guidance expects role-based and restricted access even inside sponsor and investigator TMFs. Kadarn therefore needs classification and redaction rules, not just file storage. citeturn17search0turn17search3turn5view3

This makes the Publication Engine even more important. It should decide whether a person can merely see that a document exists, preview its metadata, open it in-browser, download it as read-only, download it watermarked, or export it in a package. It should also decide whether the user sees the current version, the archived version, or both. EMA explicitly recommends read-only access to archived eTMF content with audit trail recording, and Part 11 requires audit trails for creation, modification, and deletion of electronic records. citeturn5view3turn4view1turn1search14

Retention is another major requirement that argues strongly against treating delivery as ad hoc emailing or generic ZIP exchange. EMA says the sponsor and investigator must archive the clinical TMF content for at least 25 years after the end of the clinical trial under the Regulation, and the UK MHRA’s current guidance likewise states retention of essential documents and participant medical files for at least 25 years from trial conclusion under the amended rules. A platform that delivers site and investigator evidence into regulated study workflows must therefore assume long-lived retention, traceability, and retrievability requirements from the start. citeturn4view0turn1search1

## Recommended changes to the draft and the best final model

The best version of the draft is not “give the sponsor the full Passport” and not “give them only a matrix.” It is:

**Passport for orientation. Matrix for triage. Evidence Package for proof. Document Vault for controlled retrieval. Generated bundles for operational handoff.**

That should be the product thesis for this block. It is the most defensible model against both regulatory expectations and market practice because it reduces duplicative burden, supports structured site capability reuse, preserves traceability, and still allows document-level access when needed. citeturn7search2turn15view0turn5view3turn14view2

Concretely, I would revise the draft in the following way. Keep the idea that Kadarn sells “institutional intelligence,” but tighten the vocabulary and architecture:

Use **Site/Facility Passport** as the high-level branded view, not as the core payload. Use **Capability and Readiness Matrix** instead of just Capability Matrix. Merge **Claim Explorer** into the Evidence Package model so there is one clear claim-to-proof experience rather than two overlapping surfaces. Keep **Document Vault**, but define it as a controlled, metadata-rich, auditable repository rather than a generic download area. Reframe **Publication Engine** as **Policy, Packaging, and Access Engine**, because its job is not only publication; it is entitlement, redaction, watermarking, expiry, logging, and export orchestration. Treat **Historical Portfolio** and **Digital Twin** as phase-two differentiation, not MVP-critical infrastructure. Those changes follow directly from the combination of GCP burden reduction, TMF traceability requirements, and cross-sponsor site-profile reuse patterns already visible in TransCelerate materials. citeturn14view2turn5view4turn5view3turn15view0

If Kadarn wants the sharpest possible MVP for this block, I would build only four deliverables first: **Passport**, **Capability and Readiness Matrix**, **Evidence Package**, and **Document Vault with Package Generator**. That would already cover the primary sponsor, CRO, vendor, and audit use cases. Everything else in the draft can layer on top of those primitives later. Given the regulations and industry precedents, that is the strongest, most practical path. citeturn6view0turn5view3turn8search2turn15view0
